Field Note 01Fleet alerting

Designing Fleet Alerts
Operators Can Trust

A practical approach to exception-driven fleet operations: define the expected activity, verify the evidence, and give each exception a response.

RY Rajat Yadav Product & interface notes
18 Nov 2026 11 min read

At 10:15, a dispatcher receives a notification: a delivery vehicle has been stationary for 15 minutes.

The vehicle could be unloading, waiting at a junction, taking an approved break, or experiencing a problem. Its tracker might also have stopped reporting while the map continued to display its last position.

The notification supplies a duration. The dispatcher still has to determine what that duration means.

This illustrative example exposes a design problem in fleet tracking: detecting a condition is only part of deciding whether someone should act. A useful exception system must connect reliable observations with operating expectations, then carry the resulting issue through investigation and resolution.

OpenVTS provides the product reference for this article through its documented notification settings and visual Workflow builder. The rule specification and exception lifecycle below are proposed design examples; they should be validated against the capabilities and integrations of the deployment being used.

01Framing

Define the Exception Before Choosing the Alert

An event and an operational exception serve different purposes.

A vehicle entering a warehouse geofence is an event. If the visit is scheduled, recording it may be sufficient. If the vehicle enters a restricted site outside its operating window, the same type of event may require intervention.

For this article, the distinction is:

LayerQuestion it answers
ObservationWhat did the device report?
ConditionWhich configured rule does the observation satisfy?
ExceptionHow does that condition depart from the expected operation?
NotificationWho needs to know, and how urgently?
ResponseWhat should the responsible person do?

The expectation must come from somewhere. It could be a trip assignment, customer appointment, approved break, route plan, site-specific dwell allowance, or explicitly configured policy.

Without that context, a rule can identify a long stop. Calling it an unexpected stop requires additional evidence.

Start by defining the decision the operator needs to make. For a delivery fleet, that might be: Does this stop threaten the next scheduled visit, and should dispatch contact the driver? That question gives the rule a purpose and identifies the information the notification must contain.

02The rule

Build One Rule That Can Be Explained

Consider a fleet that wants dispatchers to review prolonged stops during assigned delivery trips.

The following is an illustrative rule specification. Its values are examples for testing, not recommended defaults or a description of OpenVTS’s internal implementation.

ElementExample specification
ScopeVehicles assigned to an active delivery trip
Stop evidenceValid observations indicate negligible movement within a small area
DurationStop evidence persists for at least 15 minutes
Expected activityExclude approved stops only within their permitted time and dwell allowance
FreshnessLatest valid observation is no more than two minutes old
Evidence gapsA gap exceeding two minutes breaks the evidence of a continuous stop
OwnershipDispatcher responsible for the assigned trip
RecoveryFresh observations confirm resumed movement for at least one minute

Each row resolves an ambiguity.

Scope prevents parked vehicles outside operating hours from becoming delivery exceptions. Expected activity separates a planned visit from an unexplained stop. Freshness prevents an old map position from being presented as evidence of a continuing condition.

The evidence-gap rule also limits what the system claims to know. If a tracker reports a stop, disappears for ten minutes, and returns at the same location, the vehicle may have remained there throughout. It may also have moved and returned. The available observations do not establish continuous stationary time.

That uncertainty should affect the rule’s result.

Stopped and idling vehicles also require separate treatment. Engine idling needs evidence that the engine is running while the vehicle is stationary. Ignition or ACC may be used as a configured proxy, but it is not universally equivalent to engine-running status. OpenVTS’s documented Over Idle notification, for example, uses ACC/ignition ON together with speed zero and identifies the need for an ACC ignition source. OpenVTS notification preferences.

03Evidence

Treat Time and Missing Data Explicitly

A tracker observation has at least two relevant times:

  • Observation time: when the device recorded the position or measurement.
  • Receipt time: when the server received it.

Suppose a vehicle stops at 10:00, resumes its journey at 10:20, and reconnects to the server at 10:30. Its stored observations may describe a genuine prolonged stop. Sending a notification that implies the vehicle is currently stopped would misrepresent the situation.

The system needs a policy for delayed data. Historical observations may update journey records and contribute to later review. Whether they should generate a current notification depends on the event, its age, and the intended response.

The two timestamps answer different freshness questions, too. A recent receipt time proves that data just arrived; it does not prove that the reported position is recent. Device timestamps also need validation for clock errors and implausible values.

A recent receipt time proves that data just arrived; it does not prove that the reported position is recent.

Missing data requires its own detection path. If a rule runs only when telemetry arrives, it cannot reliably detect a tracker that has gone silent. A scheduled evaluation or timeout must compare the last valid report with the reporting behavior expected for that device.

Expected behavior matters: a tracker configured to report infrequently while parked should not be assessed against a moving vehicle’s reporting interval.

When observations become stale, represent the affected vehicle condition as unknown. Preserve the last reported position and its timestamp, but stop treating it as proof of current activity. An existing exception should remain unresolved until evidence or an authorized operational decision closes it.

04Lifecycle

Give Each Exception a Lifecycle

A vehicle reporting every 30 seconds could satisfy the same stop rule repeatedly. Those reports should normally update one continuing issue rather than generate a new interruption each time.

Separate the condition’s lifecycle from the operator’s response:

DimensionExample states
ConditionPending confirmation, active, recovered, unknown
ResponseUnassigned, assigned, acknowledged, investigating, closed

A dispatcher can acknowledge an exception while the vehicle remains stopped. Conversely, the vehicle can resume moving before anyone acknowledges the notification.

Preserving both dimensions makes the record useful. It shows what happened to the vehicle and how the organization responded.

Repeated notifications need an explicit policy. Send the initial notification when the exception qualifies. Send another when responsibility changes, escalation becomes necessary, or material new information arrives. A timer-based reminder may be appropriate, but its purpose should be defined.

Recovery needs similar care. One noisy position should not close an issue and allow the next report to reopen it. A short confirmation period or a different recovery threshold can reduce this repeated switching. The appropriate margin depends on the signal and operating conditions.

Every notification should carry enough information for the owner to begin work:

  • Vehicle and assigned trip.
  • Condition detected and qualifying duration.
  • Observation time and data age.
  • Relevant planned stop or route context.
  • A link to the supporting history.
  • The expected first action.

A successful delivery to a phone or inbox confirms only that delivery step. It does not establish acknowledgement, investigation, or resolution.

The principle is consistent with established alerting practice: urgent interruptions should be actionable, and redundant notifications consume attention that operators need for real problems. Google’s SRE guidance provides a useful reference for making those distinctions. Monitoring Distributed Systems.

“A rule can identify a long stop. Calling it an unexpected stop requires additional evidence.”

05Verification

Test the Cases That Could Mislead Dispatch

A rule is ready for a pilot when its expected behavior can be demonstrated under normal conditions and failure conditions.

For the unexpected-stop example, a useful test set includes:

Test caseExpected behavior
Approved unloading stop within its allowanceRecord the activity without raising an unexpected-stop exception
Unplanned stop with fresh, continuous evidenceRaise one exception after the configured duration
Tracker disconnects during the stopMark the condition uncertain and evaluate the separate freshness rule
Old observations arrive after the trip resumesUpdate history without presenting the old stop as a current condition
Duplicate observations are receivedAvoid creating duplicate exception records
One brief movement reading appearsApply the recovery policy before clearing the condition
Vehicle belongs to another customer accountPrevent unauthorized access to the exception and its supporting data
Notification delivery failsPreserve the issue and make the delivery failure visible

Also test how external systems behave when an integration retries a request. Repeating the same exception update should not create several dispatch tasks or customer messages. Carrying a stable exception identifier lets the receiving system recognize an update to an existing issue.

Begin with a small, representative fleet. Include scheduled stops, poor coverage, different tracker configurations, and actual dispatcher handovers. Record incorrect and missed detections alongside successful ones.

Where practical, evaluate proposed rules without sending operational notifications first. That gives the team a chance to compare the generated exceptions with what actually happened before asking dispatchers to rely on them.

06Implementation

Applying the Approach with OpenVTS

OpenVTS provides documented building blocks for this work. Its notification settings cover overspeed, duration, geofence, and route conditions. The Workflow documentation describes incoming-data and scheduled triggers, conditional logic, actions, and execution inspection.

A bounded implementation can begin with a supported condition, select the relevant vehicles, configure the required delivery service, and inspect execution results during testing. The workflow guide also distinguishes a draft from a published workflow: publishing activates the validated server version while the workflow runtime is available. OpenVTS workflow documentation.

The complete operational process may extend beyond that configuration. Trip expectations, acknowledgement, escalation, and issue closure could involve a dispatch system, service desk, integration, or documented human procedure. Their availability should be verified for the intended deployment.

Assign ownership across those systems. If the TMS owns appointments, use its appointment record as the expectation. If a service desk owns response status, keep acknowledgement there and connect it to the fleet exception through a stable identifier.

This avoids competing versions of the same operational issue.

07Measurement

Measure Reliability and Operator Workload Together

Alert volume is useful context, but it cannot establish whether exception management is improving. Fewer notifications could indicate better filtering or missed incidents.

Evaluate several measures together:

  • Detection delay: elapsed time from the condition qualifying to exception creation.
  • Acknowledgement delay: elapsed time from creation to explicit human acknowledgement.
  • Actionable-alert proportion: the share of reviewed alerts that required the intended intervention.
  • Missed exceptions: known qualifying cases that the system failed to raise.
  • Observation freshness: age of valid device observations when decisions were made.

Keep delayed telemetry separate from processing delay. Define acknowledgement through an explicit action rather than assuming that a notification was read.

Review distributions and individual failures alongside averages. A small number of severely delayed alerts may matter more than a good overall mean. Record the review sample and operating conditions so comparisons remain meaningful.

The unexpected-stop rule is successful when dispatchers can understand why it fired, assess the evidence, and take responsibility for the next step. It also needs to expose when the evidence is insufficient.

Before enabling any fleet alert, the engineering and operations teams should be able to explain its scope, qualifying condition, data requirements, owner, recovery rule, and failure behavior. Those decisions provide a sound basis for an exception-driven operation—and a concrete way to test whether it deserves the operator’s trust.
